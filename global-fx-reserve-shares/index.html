About these statistics

This is the methodology note from the accompanying report, reproduced here.

The three lines on each chart. IMF COFER, as published, is the official series, which begins in 1995. JMA, Annual Reports plus COFER is our own series, carried back to 1980 and identical to the published one from 2000. At constant end-2025 rates is that same series with exchange-rate movements stripped out.

Where the numbers come from. The IMF publishes a detailed statistic on FX reserves, Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves (COFER), with an estimate of the currency composition of the global FX reserve total. But COFER only starts in 1995, and it has a “currency unknown” part of FX reserves in 1995-1999 that the IMF only starts to allocate into currencies from 2000. Our series extends the statistics back, drawing mainly on the currency-composition tables in successive editions of the IMF's Annual Reports, supplemented by BIS statistics and national central-bank disclosures, which we used to test our reading of the “currency unknown” portion.

Two adjustments. First, before the euro, European central banks held ECUs, claims backed by gold and US dollars. We count the dollar-backed part of the ECU as USD and drop the gold-backed part, as gold is not foreign exchange. Second, exchange rates distort shares. When the yen falls against the dollar, the yen's share in global FX reserves shrinks even if nobody sells a single yen off reserves. We therefore also compute shares with every year's holdings valued at end-2025 exchange rates, using BIS year-end rates. The ECB publishes the euro's share of global FX reserves in the same manner in its annual report, The International Role of the Euro (ECB, 2026). On this constant-exchange-rate (CER) basis, movements reflect changes in the amounts held, not in their valuation.

Year by year. The same logic works year by year. For each year we estimate how the change in exchange rates would move the share, and read the difference from the actual change as what reserve managers added or shed. In 2022, for example, the dollar's appreciation alone would have raised its share by 1.8 points, but the share fell 0.5 points, implying managers shed about 2.2% of global FX reserves worth of USD that year. These estimates strip out exchange-rate effects only; reinvested interest income stays inside them. Our year-by-year decomposition follows the approach of Arslanalp, Eichengreen and Simpson-Bell (2022).

Basis. All shares are shares of total global FX reserves, including the “currency unknown” portion. Full details, sources, and limitations will follow in a separate methodology report.
