Shares of Global FX Reserves — the dollar, the euro and the yen

Data vintage: IMF COFER annual through 2025. The constant-rate series sits on a fixed end-2025 base: adding a year does not restate history, and any future rebasing will be published as a new edition rather than a silent revision.

The IMF publishes the currency composition of world reserves in COFER, but only from 1995, and until its own imputation begins in 2000 it publishes those shares against the allocated part of reserves rather than the whole. These pages extend the record back to 1980 using the currency-composition tables in successive editions of the IMF's Annual Reports, and set beside it a third measure that separates what reserve managers did from what exchange rates did to them. On each of the first three charts the published series and ours are drawn together, so where they agree and where they differ is visible rather than asserted.

The window is 2000 to 2025 because both endpoints sit inside COFER, so the split needs no adjustment for the change of source before it. Read across: the dollar's 13.3-point fall is almost all real reallocation, the euro's 2.8-point rise is entirely valuation and conceals net selling, and the yen's small observed decline masks real buying offset by a weaker currency. Over the longer 1975–2025 window the dollar's decline is a smaller share real, around two-thirds to seventy per cent, because the 1980s were dominated by valuation swings.

About these statistics

Each currency is drawn three ways, because a share can move for three different reasons — because the IMF changed what it counts, because reserve managers bought or sold, or because exchange rates moved. Only the three lines together separate them.

The three lines. IMF COFER, as published, is the official series. It begins in 1995, and until 2000 it expresses each currency as a share of the allocated part of reserves — the part whose composition countries disclosed — rather than of all reserves. JMA, Annual Reports plus COFER is our own series: the same object carried back to 1980 from the currency-composition tables of the IMF's Annual Reports, and always expressed as a share of total reserves. Constant end-2025 exchange rates takes our series and values every year's holdings at one set of exchange rates, so a movement in it reflects a change in the amounts held rather than in their price.

Why the first two lines separate before 2000, and not after. From 2000 they are the same series. The IMF now imputes the holdings of countries that do not report, so its published share is a share of everything — the same object we measure — and our panel simply carries the published numbers, stored to two decimals. Four years out of twenty-six sit on a rounding boundary and so differ by 0.1 at the one decimal we publish; that is rounding, not disagreement. Before 2000 they differ for two reasons pulling in opposite directions. Our series folds the dollar-backed portion of the old European Currency Unit into the dollar, which raises our dollar above the published one in the mid-1990s. The published series divides by allocated reserves only, which was 74% of the total in 1995, and that concentration raises the published dollar above ours by 1998, once the ECU had been wound down. The lines therefore cross around 1997. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions, and the labels say which.

Why our dollar also differs from the printed Annual Report. For 1997 our dollar share reads 66.8% against the 62.2% printed in the Annual Report for the same year. The same two adjustments explain it: the ECU look-through, and expressing every year as a share of all official holdings rather than only those of countries that reported to the Fund. By 1998 the gap narrows to 0.6 points, because the ECU had almost gone.

The end-2025 base. The constant-rate series values all history at end-2025 exchange rates, which has two consequences worth knowing. Its final point equals the observed 2025 share exactly, so the series can be checked against published COFER at its own endpoint. And the base is fixed rather than rolling: next year's observation will be priced at end-2025 rates too, so no published number changes as the series extends. If we ever move the base, it will be announced as a new edition. The choice of base matters more than it looks — moving it back a single year shifts the dollar's history by about 1.9 points on average, and the euro's by about the same, though the yen's by only 0.1.

The range on the dollar. The portion of reserves the Fund does not attribute to a named currency has no exchange rate of its own, and two defensible treatments bracket it: leaving it unrevalued, or revaluing it at the average of the named currencies. We publish both, and the shaded band on the dollar's chart is the range between them. It matters for the dollar, where the two treatments differ by up to 2.2 points on the level, and barely at all for the euro (0.2) or the yen (0.3). Headline figures should quote the range, not one rule.

Precision, coverage, and what is not here. Levels and flows are published to one decimal, the precision the pre-2000 printed sources support; the 2000–2025 decomposition draws only on COFER-era data and is shown to two. The series stop at end-2025. They cover three currencies only, start in 1980, and carry no separate line for the unattributed residual. Our wider research on how that residual might be allocated is deliberately not published here: it is a modelled estimate, and these pages carry measurement.
